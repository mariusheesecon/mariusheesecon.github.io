I am an economist working on urban, spatial, environmental, and development economics. My research studies how market access, institutional capacity, and beliefs shape economies' resilience to shocks, their path to structural transformation, and their potential for economic growth. Using structural spatial general-equilibrium models, econometric spatial methods with satellite and micro data, and pre-registered field experiments, I work on urban labor markets and informality, the measurement of local climate shocks, and the beliefs governing climate adaptation, with extensions to housing, insurance, migration, trade, and infrastructure.

I grew up in southern Germany, and life since has led me through Hamburg, Munich, Vancouver, Oxford, Boston, Chicago, Bangkok, and Copenhagen to Rotterdam. The people, cultures, societies, and histories I got to experience along the way have sparked my curiosity to understand what makes, breaks, and shapes the economies and places we live in, and inspire the questions I study. Job Market Paper

Segmented Labor Markets and Structural Transformation

Working draft (PDF), September 2026 · Online appendix (PDF). Presented at the North American Meeting of the Urban Economics Association (Chicago, 2026) and the Dutch Urban Economics and Trade Workshop (2025, 2026).

Most urban growth this century will occur in developing economies. Whether this urbanization translates into sustained economic development depends on how quickly workers gain access to productive formal employment. I develop a dynamic spatial general equilibrium model with three types of labor markets: rural, urban informal, and urban formal. Migrants enter an informal queue, and formalization depends on informal labor congestion and the economy's ability to move informal workers into formal employment. I quantify the model for Ethiopia using novel geospatial and microdata. The speed of transition from informal to formal labor markets emerges as a key determinant of long-run structural transformation, economic growth and welfare: improvements to formalization raise the long-run urban formal labor share by 26 pp and real GDP by 13.1 percent relative to the baseline, while leaving the urban population share largely unchanged. The formalization rate also shapes both the effectiveness of economic policies and national resilience to climate shocks and conflict. Cities become engines of structural transformation not when workers arrive, but when labor markets absorb them into productive employment.

Research

Two further working papers study the measurement of local weather shocks and how beliefs shape climate adaptation:

(Mis)measuring Local Weather Shocks: Evidence from Drought and Migration in Ethiopia (with Michiel Gerritse and Frank van Oort; under review) — aggregated exposure measures systematically understate the local impacts of drought, and a simple correction recovers them. Working paper (PDF)

Memory, Expectations, and Climate Adaptation (with Frank van Oort; pre-registered field experiment with 686 farmers in eastern Uganda) — compressed weather memories suppress demand for protection, and simple visual information corrects beliefs. Working draft (PDF)
