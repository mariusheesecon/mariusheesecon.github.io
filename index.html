I am an economist working on urban, spatial, environmental, and development economics. My research studies how space and networks shape economic activity, and how these dynamics, through migration, trade, infrastructure, and climate adaptation, reshape economic outcomes in developing and high-income economies. I combine structural spatial general-equilibrium models, pre-registered field experiments, and spatial methods with satellite and micro data.

I grew up in southern Germany, and life since has led me through Hamburg, Munich, Vancouver, Oxford, Boston, Chicago, Bangkok, and Copenhagen to Rotterdam. The people, cultures, societies, and histories I got to experience along the way have sparked my curiosity to understand what makes, breaks, and shapes the economies and places we live in, and inspire the questions I study. Job Market Paper

Segmented Labor Markets and Structural Transformation

Draft available on request. Presented at the North American Meeting of the Urban Economics Association (Chicago, 2026) and the Dutch Urban Economics and Trade Workshop (2025, 2026).

Urbanization without formalization is a major barrier to structural transformation and sustained growth in many developing economies. As workers move to cities, many cannot access productive formal employment and remain stuck in low-productivity informal work, reducing the gains from urban migration. This paper quantifies how formalization shapes growth, welfare and distribution. I develop a dynamic spatial equilibrium model with segmented labor markets in which workers move between rural agriculture, urban informal employment and urban formal employment, and access to formal work is constrained by the capacity of firms and institutions to absorb informal workers. When this capacity is weak, migration enlarges the informal labor pool, slows formalization, and can generate a self-reinforcing informality trap. Quantifying the model for Ethiopia shows that limited access to formal employment constrains structural transformation: raising formalization capacity increases long-run GDP by 13.1 percent, national welfare by 9.7 percent, and the urban formal employment share from 41.1 to 67.5 percent. Counterfactuals show that access to formal employment shapes policy effectiveness and distribution, generating sharply different effects across workers within the same city that single-labor-market models miss. The framework applies whenever access to a location does not imply access to all of its labor markets.

Research

Two further working papers study the measurement of local weather shocks and how beliefs shape climate adaptation:

(Mis)measuring Local Weather Shocks: Evidence from Drought and Migration in Ethiopia (with Michiel Gerritse and Frank van Oort; under review) — aggregated exposure measures systematically understate the local impacts of drought, and a simple correction recovers them.

Memory, Expectations, and Climate Adaptation (with Frank van Oort; pre-registered field experiment with 686 farmers in eastern Uganda) — compressed weather memories suppress demand for protection, and simple visual information corrects beliefs.
